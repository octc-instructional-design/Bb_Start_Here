Discussion Boards

A discussion works best when you give classmates something to think about and respond to. Follow your instructor’s prompt, rubric, and posting deadlines first.

  • Explain your thinking. Answer the prompt and connect your ideas to a reading, a specific example, or an experience. Cite sources as directed.
  • Build on classmates’ ideas. Refer to a particular point, explain why you agree or see it differently, and add an example, connection, or follow-up question.
  • Return to the conversation. Post early enough to leave room for discussion, then check back and respond to questions. Check for separate deadlines for your first post and replies.

End with a question

When it fits the assignment, end your first post with a specific, open-ended question. It’s a small courtesy: your classmates have a clear place to start, and their replies can take the conversation somewhere new.

Try: “I think this strategy would work well for small groups. What might you change if the group were much larger?”

Questions that invite conversation

  • “How might this approach work differently in a workplace?”
  • “What example would challenge this idea?”
  • “Which part of this solution would be hardest to put into practice, and why?”

A reply that adds something

“Your point about setting small goals stood out to me. Breaking a project into weekly tasks has helped me get started, but I sometimes underestimate how long each task takes. How would you adjust this strategy when a deadline changes?”

Ending with a question is a useful conversation habit, not an extra course requirement unless your instructor asks for it.
